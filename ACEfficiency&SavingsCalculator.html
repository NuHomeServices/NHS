How these numbers work

Conservative, sourced, and built for the real California retrofit home — nothing inflated.
The old unit's SEER13 → 8.2
California units from the last 10–20 years were rated 10–14 SEER when new (10 SEER min before 2006, 13 from 2006, 14 from 2015) — set your unit's original rating above (we default to 13, the typical average). Real-world efficiency then drops with age — DOE uses 3%/year; an FSEC field study measured 5.2%/year in hot-climate homes. We apply 3%/yr if never serviced or 1.7%/yr if serviced yearly, floored at 6 SEER, to land today's estimated effective SEER.
Cooling-hours, not "hours a day"1,400 EFLH
A unit can run 12–18 hours on a peak July afternoon and near-zero in winter, so "hours per day" is misleading. The math uses Equivalent Full-Load Hours (EFLH) — the hours per year the AC would run at 100% to deliver the whole season's cooling, a standard HVAC sizing metric. We anchor EFLH to NOAA cooling-degree-day (CDD) normals: Central Valley (Fresno ~1,670, Bakersfield ~2,000–2,260 CDD) → ~1,400 EFLH; inland Southern California (Riverside ~1,400, San Bernardino ~1,550 CDD) → ~1,200 EFLH. Cooling season runs ~May–October, with hard use June–September (Fresno averages 4 months above 85°F).
Why Central > Southern
The Central Valley has no marine air — summer highs sit 91–98°F for four-plus months and nights stay warm, so it cools harder and longer. Populated inland Southern California (the Inland Empire) gets overnight marine relief, so its degree-days — and therefore cooling-hours — run lower. That's why Central shows more cooling-hours than Southern. (Desert SoCal like Palm Springs is the exception and runs even higher.)
Utility rates
Central CA (PG&E) ≈ 40¢/kWh (actually ~42¢ as of March 2026); Southern CA (SCE) ≈ 35¢/kWh (~34.5¢) — both verified and slightly conservative. The 15-year view escalates rates 4%/year, in line with recent California trends.
The new units
Comfort = 14 SEER2. Comfort + = 16 SEER2 — your Comfort + stock runs 15–17 SEER2 depending on market availability, so we model the 16 average. Both are single-stage. New units are rated on the stricter SEER2 test (~5% tougher than the old SEER the old unit was rated on), so the real efficiency gap is a touch larger than the bars show — the comparison favors the customer, conservatively.
Sources: U.S. DOE & FSEC/UCF (efficiency degradation); EPA / DOE (dirty-coil losses); NOAA cooling-degree-day normals (ggweather.com, 1981–2010); PG&E & SCE 2026 residential rates. Model assumptions: original SEER 10–14 (default 13), degradation 3%/yr (unserviced) or 1.7%/yr (serviced) floored at 6 SEER; new units degrade 2%/yr; 4%/yr utility escalation; EFLH 1,400 (Central) / 1,200 (inland SoCal). Estimates for comparison based on DOE/LBNL degradation factors; actual efficiency varies — a Manual J / field test gives the exact figure for any specific home.
